2.1 Few-Shot & Contextual Prompting Basics

Language models excel when given contextual framing (defining the persona, tone, goal, and constraints). In addition, **Few-shot Prompting** provides concrete input-output examples directly inside the context window, showing the model "how" to behave rather than just explaining it.

Example: Contextual Blog Generator
Prompt:
Context: You are writing for a blog about retro 80's arcade video games. Suggest 3 topics to write an article about with a few lines of description of what this article should contain.

Output:
1. The Evolution of Arcade Cabinet Design: From early wood cabinets in the 70s to sleek, neon-lit cabinets of the 80s.
2. Blast from the Past: Iconic Arcade Games of the 80s: Historical legacy and mechanics of the most influential arcade machines.
3. The Rise and Retro Revival of Pixel Art: Exploring pixel art as a defining retro visual style.

2.2 Step-Back Prompting Reasoning

Step-back prompting forces the model to take a step back from a complex, highly detailed prompt, consider a broader, underlying principle or general question first, and then use that general background knowledge to solve the specific task.

This mitigates bias and activates relevant parameters in the LLM's weights before attempting the target task. Consider this comparison:

Traditional Query

"Write a one paragraph storyline for a new level of a first-person shooter game that is challenging and engaging."

⚠️ Generates highly generic, random storylines (e.g. squad ambushed in dense urban alleys) because it lacks conceptual bounding.
Step-Back Query

Step 1: "What are 5 fictional key settings that contribute to a challenging FPS storyline?" → (Yields: Abandoned Military Base, Cyberpunk City, Deep Sea Lab, etc.)

Step 2: "Choose one setting from above and write a level storyline."

✓ Yields highly detailed, rich narratives (e.g., deep-sea laboratory, high pressure, mutated aquatic monstrosities).

2.3 Chain of Thought (CoT) Prompting Math

Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting improves reasoning by generating intermediate steps. Instead of expecting the neural network to output the final answer immediately (which forces it to compress calculations into a single prediction frame), we prompt it to "think step by step."

This is highly visible in math reasoning, where LLMs frequently fail due to their training characteristics:

Without CoT

Prompt: "When I was 3 years old, my partner was 3 times my age. Now, I am 20 years old. How old is my partner?"

Output: 63 years old

❌ Fails math check: multiplying 3 * 20 without tracking the difference.
With Zero-Shot CoT

Prompt: "When I was 3, my partner was 3 times my age. Now I am 20. How old is my partner? **Let's think step by step.**"

1. My partner was 3 * 3 = 9 years old when I was 3.
2. The age difference is 9 - 3 = 6 years (partner is older).
3. Since I am 20 now, partner is 20 + 6 = 26.

Output: 26 years old

✓ Succeeds by outlining intermediate variables.

Combining CoT with Single-Shot / Few-Shot

CoT is even more robust when you provide an example reasoning chain in the prompt context:

Prompt:
Q: When my brother was 2, I was double his age. Now I am 40. How old is my brother? Let's think step by step.
A: When my brother was 2, I was 2 * 2 = 4 years old (2 years older). Now I am 40, so my brother is 40 - 2 = 38. Answer: 38.

2.4 Self-Consistency (Majority Voting) Voting

CoT uses a simple "greedy decoding" path, which can still lead to a logical slip. **Self-Consistency** addresses this by generating a diverse set of reasoning paths (using a higher temperature) and applying a **majority vote** over the final answers.

Case Study: Security Email Classification
Consider an email reporting a bug in a WordPress contact form: the sender claims to have triggered a JavaScript alert box, signed by "Harry the Hacker" but stating "feel free to leave the bug, it gives me interesting things to read."

Because of the sarcastic tone and lack of clear request, a single greedy path might get tricked.

Self-Consistency Execution:
  • Attempt 1: Identifies SQL/XSS exploit impact. → IMPORTANT
  • Attempt 2: Notes lack of personal urgency and sarcastic tone. → NOT IMPORTANT
  • Attempt 3: Identifies serious vulnerability risk and unknown sender credibility. → IMPORTANT

Result: By running the prompt 3 times at $T = 0.7$, the majority vote determines the final output to be IMPORTANT, providing a much higher probability of correctness.

2.5 Tree of Thoughts (ToT) Trees

While Chain of Thought solves linear problems, many real-world tasks require exploration, strategic lookahead, and backtrack planning. **Tree of Thoughts (ToT)** generalizes CoT by structuring intermediate steps as branches on a tree. The model can explore multiple paths, evaluate its progress at each node, and backtrack if a path leads to a dead-end.

2.6 ReAct Orchestration (Reason + Act) Agents

Reason and Act (ReAct) prompting combines natural language reasoning with external tools (such as search engines or database APIs). It models a continuous "thought-action-observation" loop, laying the foundation for autonomous agentic behaviors.

The model first writes a **Thought** detailing its strategy, selects a tool **Action** and **Action Input**, waits for the execution result (**Observation**), and feeds this observation back into its prompt context to trigger the next loop iteration.

LangChain ReAct Agent with Google Vertex AI

This implementation sets up a ReAct loop using `langchain` and `SerpAPI` to fetch real-time search results:

from langchain.agents import load_tools
from langchain.agents import initialize_agent
from langchain.agents import AgentType
from langchain.llms import VertexAI

# 1. Instantiate the LLM
llm = VertexAI(temperature=0.1)

# 2. Load search engine SerpAPI tool
tools = load_tools(["serpapi"], llm=llm)

# 3. Initialize ReAct Agent
agent = initialize_agent(
    tools=tools, 
    llm=llm, 
    agent=AgentType.ZERO_SHOT_REACT_DESCRIPTION, 
    verbose=True
)

# 4. Execute planning query
prompt = "How many kids do the band members of Metallica have?"
agent.run(prompt)
